1. Describe patterns and beliefs underlying abusive thinking, behaviors, and choices made.

  

Abusive thinking often relies on distorted beliefs about power, entitlement, and control. The ideas that other people’s needs don’t matter, that anger or force is justified, or that your behavior is not your responsibility. These beliefs can shape choices that reduce accountability (blaming, minimizing, or rationalizing), limit a person’s ability to empathize, and treat respect as optional. Over time, the behaviors that follow may become patterned: seeking dominance, escalating to intimidate or control, using manipulation or coercion to get needs met, and avoiding responsibility for harm. Recognizing these patterns helps a person interrupt the cycle by identifying triggers, and challenging the underlying beliefs.

  

2. Detail controlling and violent behaviors without blame, minimization, or denial.

  

Accurately naming controlling and violent behaviors helps the person demonstrate clear accountability and understanding of what abusive patterns look like. It also supports safety: people can’t interrupt a cycle they can’t identify, and identifying these behaviors by description (rather than blame or denial) shows readiness to change choices, manage triggers, and prevent harm going forward.

  

3. Describe the effect of abusive behavior on others, from their point of view – using examples.

  

It shows the person can understand how abusive actions affect safety, freedom, emotions, and daily choices from what the other person experiences. Using examples makes sure they’re not giving vague statements; they can link specific behaviors to real, concrete consequences—an essential step for preventing repetition and for making repair and change believable. This helps prove you understand the harm and are ready to change.
