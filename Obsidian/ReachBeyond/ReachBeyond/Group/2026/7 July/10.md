## What is the most important lesson you learned about shame?

Near the end, she said we're not broken. We're all just fine. That doesn't meant here isn't room for change, but we did what we could with the tools we had at the time. When the only tool you have is a hammer, everything looks like a nail. Dealing with shame in whatever way - e.g. anger, fear, depression, anxiety, perfectionism, people pleasing - has been that tool. She said many people living with shame were "strong", but that strength, the tools they developed with that strength, were directed into keeping shame in the shadows. People living whole lives aren't hiding from it, they've developed tools to live with it productively. They learned to live with this part of being human and how to set boundaries around it. They understand how to honor their own process. Not by oversharing with strangers, but by sharing vulnerability with the people who have earned it. They have learned to be seen without using their shame to manipulate or seek validation.

By sharing with someone who was not willing, or able to sit in the dark and accept the story with kindness, this can lead to deeper shame because the other person cannot respect the vulnerability ot took to share that story.

  

## How can talking openly about shame reduce its power?  
Shame thrives in secrecy. Where guilt is a natural emotion that occurs when we feel remorse for something we've done and can catalyze change, shame corrodes the belief that we can change at all. Giving a name to shame and shining a light into the shadows shows allows us where the boundaries are. By identifying the boundaries of our shame, we are able to work through the negative self talk that enables and deepens shame, and leaves us stuck. Shame thrives in secrecy and we take that secrecy away when we give it a name.
